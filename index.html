Introduction

The Hearth Kitchen

This building is a reconstruction of the Hearth Kitchen that was added to the house by James Crane, the son of Israel, in the 1800s.

The reconstruction was completed using photographs and drawings of the original Hearth Kitchen.

Starting the Fire

How did the Crane family start their fire?

In the time of the Crane family, they would have used flint and steel to start their fire.

Ideally, they would never let their fire completely die out. They would make sure at least some coals kept nice and hot so that they could get the fire started up again — because using flint and steel is not easy.

Who Cooked

Who would have been cooking in the kitchen?

The Crane family had hired workers and at least two enslaved workers, Dine and Joe. Dine, as a woman, would have done the cooking and cleaning for the family.

She would have been the one to work all day to keep the fire going and to cook all the food for Israel Crane and his family.

1 of 3 — Ways to Cook

Over the fire

Pots hanging on the crane directly over the fire. Good for soups and hot water for tea.

2 of 3 — Ways to Cook

On the hearth stones

First, they would pull out some coals from the fire and place them on the stone, then set the spider pan on top of the coals.

To make the pan hotter, they would add more coals; to cool it, they would take some away — like an older version of our stove-top today. Good for cooking eggs.

3 of 3 — Ways to Cook

The reflector oven

A reflective oven would get placed right next to the fire — the heat reflects off the back part and helps cook the food all around.

Good for hanging sausages or birds (the reflector oven with the hooks), or for cookies (the reflector oven with the sheet).
